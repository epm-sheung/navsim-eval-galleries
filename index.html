What our training loss actually measures

Four investigations into the gap between the objective we optimise and the benchmark we are judged on. The short version: they are close to unrelated, and the one thing the loss tracks best is the one thing that cannot change the score.

How the comparison was made

The question is whether a scene our model finds easy (low training loss) is also a scene the benchmark rewards (high PDMS). Both quantities had to exist for the same 12,146 scenes before they could be compared.

  1. Dump every prediction. For each of 6 models we ran the agent over the full navtest split and saved its predicted trajectory per scene — 12,146 each. These are the same poses the official scorer consumed, so no frame-convention mismatch can creep in.
  2. Recompute the training loss per scene. Not the loss in the current config — the one that actually trained these checkpoints: 0.7·latent_L1 + 0.3·scaled_Huber, where the latent term is an L1 in a frozen 4-dimensional whitened-velocity autoencoder space. Ground-truth futures came from the scene loader, since no converted navtest set exists.
  3. Take the metric per scene from the official eval CSVs — the total PDMS and all six subscores, already on disk from the scoring runs.
  4. Rank-correlate the two. Spearman and Kendall, which compare orderings rather than raw values — appropriate here because loss is in arbitrary units and PDMS is bounded 0–1. Negative is the expected direction: lower loss should mean higher score.
  5. Split out the ceiling. 26.8% of scenes score exactly 1.000. That tie block distorts any rank statistic, so every figure is reported twice — over all scenes, and over the interior only, where 0 < score < 1 and the scenes actually discriminate.

What could have invalidated this, and why it didn't

Every model's per-scene scores were averaged back up and checked against its published official score. All six matched to better than 1e−14 (largest error 2.22e−16). If the join between predictions and scores had been wrong — misaligned tokens, dropped rows — that check would have failed. It is the reason these correlations can be read as real rather than as an artefact of bad plumbing.

One honest limitation. The trainer's huber_scale is a per-batch detached scalar — it has no per-sample value. We used a single global mean(latent)/mean(huber) per model, the same convention the existing PAV scorer uses. This changes the loss's absolute scale but not the ordering of scenes within a model, which is all a rank correlation depends on.

1 · The loss does not predict the metric

How to read these numbers. Spearman ρ measures whether two rankings agree — scenes ordered by lowest loss, against scenes ordered by highest score.

ρcloser to −1 is betterWhat it would mean
−1.00The loss ranks scenes exactly as the metric does. A perfect predictor.
 0.00The loss tells you nothing about the score.
+1.00The loss ranks scenes exactly backwards — the scenes it fits best are the ones it drives worst.

Everything below sits between −0.24 and +0.05.

The loss tracks the one component that cannot move the score

Read the chart top to bottom. Driving direction compliance has the strongest correlation — and carries driving_direction_weight = 0.0 in v1. It is computed, reported, and completely inert.

Below it, ego progress flips positive: on the scenes that discriminate, lower loss goes with less progress. Ego progress is weighted 5⁄12, joint-largest. Fitting the expert trajectory more tightly is mildly associated with getting less far.

Precision matters here: −0.215 is the least weak correlation, not a strong one. The honest summary is not "the loss predicts driving direction" but "the loss predicts nothing well, and what it predicts least badly is inert."

Two heavy components, pulling opposite ways

Time-to-collision is the strongest correlation with anything that counts (−0.191), and it is nearly as strong as the inert one. That is the loss working as designed: 70% of its weight is a velocity-profile term, and TTC is fundamentally a question about speed and spacing.

But TTC and ego progress carry identical weight — 5⁄12 each — and the loss goes opposite ways on them. It predicts safety-by-timing correctly while leaning against getting down the road. Two effects of similar size, cancelling.

That reframes the near-zero headline. The loss is not uniformly useless; it has one half of what it needs. Nothing in the objective rewards progress, so a trajectory that hugs the expert's velocity profile scores well on loss whether or not it actually advances.

That last point is a genuine, if modest, vindication of the loss design. The velocity-latent term is earning its place — it is simply a better version of a weak predictor. It is also the first direct confirmation on NAVSIM that ADE is weak here, rather than an inference carried over from the PAV dataset.

Constant velocity, read carefully. Its full-set ρ of −0.029 against the learned models' −0.22 to −0.31 is the meaningful comparison: a model with no learned behaviour carries no signal, so the learned models' weak-but-real correlation is a property of the models rather than of the metric. Its interior +0.378 should not be read as a clean contrast — constant velocity scores exactly zero on 8,130 of 12,146 scenes and never reaches 1.0, so its interior is only 4,016 unrepresentative scenes: the ones where blind velocity extrapolation half-works.

Scope. This is a within-model, per-scene correlation. The frequently-cited −0.36 / +0.90 (arXiv:2605.00066) is a between-model correlation over 8 published methods — one point per method. Different unit of analysis; this neither reproduces nor contradicts it.

2 · Seed variance is larger than most of our conclusions

A seed fixes the randomness in training — weight initialisation, batch order, nondeterministic GPU arithmetic. Same seed, same result. We retrained Variant 1 changing only the seed, holding code, data, recipe and environment identical.

What this puts in doubt

Against the 3.62-point navhard spread:

  • SAE B′ vs A′ — 1.82 points. Inside noise. This is the comparison the entire SAE arm rests on, and the ordering that reversed after the sensor-path fix. On one run each, it is not established.
  • Variant 1 vs SAE B′ — 2.94 points. Inside noise.
  • Variant 1 vs cmd_tokens_film — 0.58 points. Well inside, on both splits.
  • Variant 1 vs cls_tokens_film — 6.87 points. Outside. Patch-vs-CLS survives, as does Variant 1 over SAE A′ at 4.76.

Two runs give a range, not a standard deviation — this bounds nothing formally. Separating the sub-4-point comparisons needs 3–5 seeds per arm, roughly 10 GPU-hours each at 1h41 per run. Until then those rows are "not separated", not ranked.

3 · Where the models actually fail

Every navtest scene characterised by speed, curvature, agent count and drivable-area margin, then grouped into the 20 score bins. Two variables separate the bins cleanly — and one runs backwards.

  • Drivable-area margin is the cleanest predictor — 2.84 m in the failure bin against 4.69 m at the top, rising almost monotonically. Scenes fail where the road is tight, and the zero bin is dominated by drivable-area gate failures.
  • Agent count runs backwards. Hardest bin averages 31.7 agents, easiest 50.4. Plausibly dense traffic implies wide multi-lane roads. Whatever the mechanism, "crowded means hard" is wrong here.
  • Speed barely separates (4.4–6.9 m/s throughout). These are not high-speed failures.
  • Two distinct failure modes. Bin 0 is 100% intrinsically hard — all six models fail together. Bins 0.70–0.85 carry a model-weakness signature, up to 74% of scenes having exactly one of six models fail. Benchmark difficulty versus a fixable gap.

4 · Turns are where we lose

Every scene clustered by its driving command. Unknown is 0.00% on navtest — the same degenerate pattern as navtrain, unlike private_test_hard's 5.45% — so it is dropped rather than reported from nothing.

Three things this says

  • Our right-turn penalty is 9.5 points; TransFuser's is 3.4 at a similar aggregate score. Tempting to credit LiDAR — but the literature prices LiDAR at +0.2 PDMS overall (camera-only LTF 83.8 vs TransFuser 84.0, NAVSIM Table 1), so that explanation is not supported. The gap is real and localised to turns; its cause is more likely the BEV representation than the sensor. Open question.
  • Constant velocity is the only model that does not degrade on turns — marginally better, because it never attempts one. The degradation is specific to models that try.
  • The human ceiling also dips (−1.9), so part of this is intrinsic difficulty. Ours is five times that, so most of it is not.

Right turns are also the rarest command — 1,575 scenes, 13.0% of the split, against 66.4% straight. Rarest and worst is suggestive, but this data cannot separate "under-represented in training" from "intrinsically harder".

What follows

  • Do not read sub-4-point differences as results without multiple seeds behind them. That includes both SAE arms.
  • The loss is a weak proxy and mildly anti-correlated with ego progress on discriminating scenes — direct on-NAVSIM evidence for metric distillation, which optimises the thing being measured rather than a velocity reconstruction that happens to correlate at −0.09.
  • Right turns and tight drivable area are the specific failure mode. A far sharper target than "improve PDMS".
  • ADE is confirmed weak on NAVSIM directly, not by analogy — it ranks worse than the training loss on every model tested.
