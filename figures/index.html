Scene clips

The same scenes as video. These are rebuilt from the gallery's own clips — the camera frames are decoded out of the existing MP4s, so no dataset access is needed — then re-composited against the corrected BEV. 1920×1080 at 20 fps, ~8.6 s, against the gallery's 1500×540 at 2 fps for 3.5 s.

What the clip adds over the gallery version

  • The trajectory is in the video. The gallery clips contain no trajectory lines at all — the viewer draws them in the browser, so the MP4 alone is nearly empty. Here the prediction is baked in, and projected into the camera image too.
  • Two phases. ~2 s of history, cross-faded to 20 fps instead of stuttering at 2, then a 4 s future rollout where the camera holds and the BEV animates at 10 Hz, with agent boxes interpolated between the nine stored timesteps.
  • Live state. Per-gate chips, a running clock, a scale bar, and the failure caption surfaced when it applies.

Downloading a frame

Press play, pause where you want, then use the buttons under the video. The arrows step one frame (1/20 s) at a time, and the readout shows the exact timestamp and frame index so a figure caption can cite it.

  • Camera (left) and BEV (right) save the two halves as separate PNGs, cropped at the renderer's own panel rectangles — the crop boxes come from scenes_v2/clip_meta.json, written by the render job, so they cannot drift out of sync with the layout.
  • Full frame saves the whole 1920×1080 composite.
  • The chips under the slider jump straight to a time point — dashed ones are in the replayed history (t < 0), solid ones in the future rollout. The chip for the segment you are in highlights as you scrub.
  • Each clip is 172 frames — 40 of replayed history (t = −1.5…0.0 s) then 132 of future rollout (t = 0.0…4.0 s), at 20 fps for 8.6 s. The slider steps one frame at a time.
  • Metadata (JSON) saves everything known about that scene: all per-gate sub-scores, PDMS for every method evaluated on it, ego speed, driving command, heading change and curvature, agent count, the failure captions, the BEV bounds, and the timestamp you were paused at.

Ordered worst PDMS first. Posters are the t = +0.6 s frame; nothing autoplays and nothing loads until you press play.

Analysis figures

What a survey of the published figures actually shows

Seven primary sources were enumerated figure-by-figure — the NAVSIM benchmark paper (arXiv:2406.15349), Hydra-MDP (2406.06978), DiffusionDrive (2411.15139), DriveSuprim (2506.06659), GTRS (2506.06664), TransFuser (2205.15997) and Bench2Drive (2406.03877). Three results shaped the choices below.

  • The multi-component score is never charted. Sub-score decomposition (NC / DAC / EP / TTC / Comfort) appears only as table columns, in all seven. No stacked bars, no radar plots, no parallel coordinates anywhere. The figure slot is open, not forbidden — the absence is plausibly page-limit economics.
  • Seed variance is reported, but never plotted. NAVSIM quotes ±0.56 PDMS over three seeds in ablation prose; TransFuser gives Table VI to a 3-seed × 3-run study. Always a table or an inline ±. A figure that draws the noise band would be a departure from convention, and arguably an improvement.
  • The correlation figure already has a canonical form. NAVSIM's own Fig. 3(a) is a per-planner scatter of open-loop against closed-loop score with fitted trendlines, and Fig. 4(a) is a grouped bar chart of Spearman and Pearson coefficients by planner type. A loss-versus-metric figure should look like those.

One hard constraint: never stack PDMS

PDMS is a gated composite, not a sum. From the benchmark paper's Eq. 1:

PDMS = NC × DAC × (5·EP + 5·TTC + 2·C) / 12

NC and DAC enter multiplicatively as hard gates; the rest is a weighted average normalised by 12. No assignment of the five sub-scores to stacked segments sums to PDMS, so a stacked bar is not merely unconventional here — it is wrong, and a reviewer who knows the metric will see it immediately. Grouped bars and small multiples are unaffected. The same gated structure holds for v2 EPDMS, so the argument survives the metric change.

2 · Ablation deltas against a seed-noise band

The figure that kills over-reading. Each model's distance from the reference, with the measured run-to-run spread drawn behind it: anything inside the band is not separated from luck.

State the scope, or this figure overclaims

This band governs separate training runs. It does not govern paired ablations evaluated on a frozen policy over the same scenes, where the pipeline reproduces to ~1e−5 and a 0.02 delta is real. A figure like this without that caption invites the reader to dismiss every paired result in the paper.

PrecedentNone as a figure. NAVSIM and TransFuser both report seed spread as a table or inline ±.
AnswersWhich of these differences are real?
NeedsAt least two runs of one config. Three to five would let you draw a real interval instead of a range.
TrapDrawing a range from two runs and calling it a standard deviation. It bounds nothing formally; say so.

4 · Oracle-versus-K curve

How much of the headroom is reachable by choosing rather than by regressing better. Plotted as a curve, which is the part nobody publishes.

The privileged-pool trap

The wider pool behind this curve contains ground-truth-derived perturbations, and quoting best-of-63 from it gives +0.194. The 40-entry vocabulary is the only unprivileged pool and it gives +0.170. Worse, the human trajectory sits at a fixed column index in every scene, so a naive “best constant column” baseline is just always pick the human. Any figure like this must say which pool it used.

PrecedentDriveSuprim Table 1 gives PDMS at K = 1, 4, 16, 256 — as a table. The curve is an unclaimed slot.
